# Lab 10 - Rocket Lab (RKLB): five-year pro-forma

## D - Question and company-specific line

What are five years of Rocket Lab's statements worth, built from assumptions I
can defend?

Rocket Lab's R&D spending must be modeled
separately because developing new launch capability costs money before it earns
revenue. On the financing side, customer contract advances replace ABG's
floor-plan line: they help finance work in progress but also represent obligations
to deliver products or services. They are not interest-bearing inventory loans.

The forecast is a scenario starting from December 31, 2025, with
FY2026-FY2030 projections. It uses the three latest annual filings and the 2025
filing's subsequent-event disclosures. It is not a reconstruction of actual 2026
results or a fully updated September investment valuation. Later acquisitions,
financing, operating results and share issuance are outside this annual baseline.
The September quote below is a dated comparison, not evidence that those later
events are incorporated.

growth rates below remain judgment and do not assume recurring free acquisitions.
ABG's video uses 1.8% instead of 4.7% because extrapolating acquired growth without

## I / V - Model mechanics, results and refusal tests

Run from the course folder:

```powershell
python rocketlab_lab10.py
python rocketlab_lab10.py --break-cash
python rocketlab_lab10.py --stress-funding
```

The last two commands intentionally raise errors. They do not edit the saved
assumptions. Normal output is also saved in `rocketlab_lab10_output.txt`.

Gross profit minus SG&A minus R&D gives operating income. Depreciation and
amortization are already included in these expense assumptions and are not
subtracted a second time. They reduce asset balances and are added back once in
cash flow. Net working capital is receivables + contract assets + inventory +
prepaids - payables - contract liabilities. Its increase uses cash.

`FCFE = net income + depreciation + amortization - change in net working capital - capex - cash debt repayment`

Cash is opening cash plus signed FCFE plus securities-sale proceeds. No cash
balance or equity balance is plugged. Noncash conversion affects debt, equity
and shares, but neither CFO nor cash. The program prints all three statements.

## Value, negative FCFE and market comparison

The [lab][lab] asks to value positive cash flows for loss-making companies. I
show that convention explicitly without deleting the negative years from the
statements or claiming they have no funding cost.

| Valuation component | USD millions |
|---|---:|
| PV of positive explicit-year FCFE | 41.741 |
| PV of sustainable terminal value | 721.171 |
| Lab positive-FCFE value | 762.912 |
| PV of negative explicit-year FCFE, separately shown | -520.725 |
| Starting cash + securities less 100 cash reserve | 998.824 |
| Separate funding-adjusted equity value | 1,241.011 |

**Lab convention: $1.23 per share**, using 619.894349 million scenario economic
shares. **Separate signed-FCFE plus excess-liquidity result: $2.00 per share.**
The latter equals initial excess liquidity plus all signed discounted FCFE plus
terminal value. Securities sales are excluded from FCFE because the securities
are already included in initial liquidity; ending cash is not added again.
Debt is not subtracted again after modeling its conversion/repayment.

Sustainable FY2031 FCFE is 174.064, using:

`2030 operating income x 1.03 x (1 - 25%) - 3% x (2030 net PP&E + 2030 net working capital)`

The terminal calculation assumes depreciation and intangible amortization are
offset by replacement spending, plus investment to grow net PP&E. It assumes
reinvestment slows from the explicit expansion period. This explains the jump
from 83.956 in 2030 FCFE to 174.064 in 2031; it is a material judgment, not a
mechanical continuation of 20% growth. Terminal value is discounted five years
using `FCFE2031 / (15% - 3%)` and provides **94.53% of the lab convention's value**.

A perpetual negative FCFE stream is not a defensible going-concern equity
terminal value: a recovery and its financing must be modeled first. The formula
can mathematically return a negative number, but that does not establish a
meaningful value for a limited-liability share. This code refuses such a base.

## Partner discussion

### Initial company explanation and question

**Explanation** Rocket Lab needs a separate R&D line because it spends
money developing launch vehicles before those projects generate revenue. Its
customer advances also affect cash: receiving payment before delivery helps fund
operations, but the company still owes the customer the product or service.

**Reviewer's Question:** If customer advances help
fund the business, why can the model still show negative FCFE?

**Answer:** Advances cover only part of the cash needs. Development
spending, equipment purchases and other working-capital investment can exceed
that funding, so the model must also track how much existing cash is consumed.

### Assumption challenge and two-sentence response

**Reviewer's Challenge:** Why should gross margin rise from
about 34% to 43% while annual capex reaches only 200 and R&D grows slowly?
What delivery or cost evidence would make you lower that assumption?

**Response:** I think Rocket Lab could improve its gross
margin as it builds and launches more products, spreading factory and equipment
costs over more sales and improving production efficiency, although 43% is my
scenario assumption rather than a company promise. I would lower that assumption
if launch delays, manufacturing overruns or higher material costs keep unit costs
from falling, and reconsider whether the model needs more capex and R&D spending.

**Reviewer's Follow-up:** That explains a possible direction, but not
why 43% is the right endpoint. Keep it labeled judgment, and check whether the
growth, margin and investment assumptions remain consistent when new evidence
arrives.

### Swap roles - challenge to a hypothetical peer model

**Peer's Company Explanation:** My hypothetical manufacturing model
has significant customer receivables, so the time customers take to pay affects
how much cash the company needs to support growth.

**My Initial Question:** If customers pay more slowly while revenue grows,
where does that appear in your statements?

**Peer's Answer:** Accounts receivable increases on the balance sheet,
and the increase reduces operating cash flow. Revenue can grow without the cash
arriving in the same period.

**Challenge:** Why does collection time improve from 50 to 30 days,
and what evidence supports customers paying 20 days faster? Would your cash
balance still meet its minimum if collection time stayed at 50 days?

**Peer's Answer:** The 30-day endpoint assumes stronger
collections and better payment terms, but without supporting contract or
historical evidence I should label it an optimistic judgment. I would use
50 days as the comparison case, recalculate receivables and cash, and only retain
the improvement if actual collections support it.

**Follow-up:** Faster collection releases cash but does not create
additional sales or profit by itself. The cash-flow benefit should be traceable
to the change in receivables and should not also be added to earnings.

### Organic-growth explanation for discussion

**Reviewer's Question:** Why does the ABG video use 1.8% growth instead
of its 4.7% reported growth, and how does that apply to Rocket Lab?

**My Explanation:** Organic growth comes from the business already owned,
while reported growth can also include acquired businesses. ABG's 1.8% video
assumption avoids treating its 4.7% reported growth as recurring growth that costs
nothing to acquire; for Rocket Lab I would likewise separate acquired revenue
from growth in its existing launch and space-systems operations.

**Reviewer's Follow-up:** Does that mean Rocket Lab's reported growth
can automatically be called organic?

**My Answer:** No. The history section identifies acquired GEOST revenue
and distinguishes a simple acquisition-adjusted calculation from an officially
reconciled organic-growth measure; the forecast rates remain judgments.

## Reflect

**Reviewer's Question:** Which assumption label would you defend the
longest, and which filing figure surprised you?

The label I would defend longest is **judgment** on margin improvement: historical
progress is evidence to consider, not a promise of the same future improvement.
One surprising figure is 2025 R&D of 270.716, larger than gross profit of 207.181;
revenue growth alone is not enough to establish profitability or positive FCFE.
**Reviewer's Response:** That distinction matters because a positive
gross margin does not guarantee positive operating income or cash flow. Keeping
R&D separate makes the cost of development visible in the model.
