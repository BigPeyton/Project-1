## 1. My conclusion — approximately 1 minute

My conclusion on Rocket Lab is watch and wait. The company has growth potential, but developing and expanding its business requires substantial spending. In my model, free cash flow to shareholders stays negative through 2029 and becomes positive in 2030.

That improvement depends on strong revenue growth, better margins and investment staying within my assumptions. My sensitivity analysis found that revenue growth had the larger effect over the ranges tested, although research and development spending also made a substantial difference.

The analysis is based on year-end 2025 financials and an annual forecast. It is not a fully updated valuation as of today. I would use these results to identify what needs more research before making an investment decision.

## 2. Why Rocket Lab and how it earns money — approximately 2 minutes

I selected Rocket Lab because it connects a growth opportunity with a difficult financial question: can a business investing heavily in technology and expansion eventually generate enough cash to support its valuation?

Rocket Lab earns revenue through launch services and space systems. That makes it useful for studying both growth and spending. More sales can help, but the company also needs development work, equipment and working capital to deliver those sales.

My initial focus was on the growth opportunity. As the analysis developed, I focused more on the spending and funding needed to achieve that growth. A growing company can still consume cash for several years.

The historical evidence covers 2023 through 2025. In 2025, Rocket Lab reported about $601.8 million in revenue and $207.2 million in gross profit. It also reported about $270.7 million in R&D and negative operating cash flow of $165.5 million.

Those numbers help explain why sales growth alone is not enough. Gross profit still has to cover expenses such as R&D and administration, and cash must also fund investment.

The underlying sources are the company's annual filings. The filings report dollars in thousands, while my model uses dollars in millions. For example, reported revenue of 601,799 thousand becomes 601.799 million. That unit conversion is important when linking the statements and calculating value per share.

## 3. How I built the financial forecast — approximately 3 minutes

[Show the Lab 10 assumption table, then `rocketlab_lab10_output.txt`.]

The forecast starts with the December 31, 2025 balance sheet and projects 2026 through 2030. I used historical information as a starting point, then separated reported facts from forecast judgments.

Revenue growth starts at 40% and slows to 20% by 2030. Gross margin rises from 35% to 43%. The economic idea is that a larger business could spread production costs over more sales and improve its mix. However, delays or cost overruns could prevent that improvement. These percentages are scenario assumptions, not company promises.

R&D rises from $290 million to $330 million per year. Capital spending rises from $160 million to $200 million. One of the most important questions is whether these spending paths are enough to support the assumed growth and margin improvement.

The statements are linked. Revenue and gross margin determine gross profit. Subtracting administration and R&D gives operating profit. After interest and taxes, net income flows into both the cash-flow statement and shareholders' equity.

To calculate free cash flow to equity, the model adds back depreciation and amortization, then subtracts working-capital investment, capital spending and cash debt repayment. Depreciation and amortization are already included in the modeled expenses, so they are not deducted a second time from operating profit.

One company-specific feature is customer advances. Customers paying before delivery can help finance operations, but Rocket Lab still owes them products or services. These advances are operating liabilities, not ordinary inventory loans.

Another feature is convertible debt. In this simplified scenario, the notes convert into shares. That changes debt, equity and the share count without creating cash. Selling existing securities makes cash available, but the sale is not operating revenue or free cash flow generated by the business.

[Point to the FCFE and ending-cash rows.]

Free cash flow to equity is approximately negative $310.7 million in 2026, negative $222.7 million in 2027, negative $116.9 million in 2028, and negative $9.3 million in 2029. It becomes positive at about $84 million in 2030.

This also shows why profit and cash flow are different. Net income becomes positive in 2028, but investment still keeps free cash flow negative that year.

The saved results show all five balance sheets balancing and cash staying above the $100 million minimum. Existing cash and securities fund the modeled losses. Passing those checks shows that the accounting works; it does not prove the business will achieve the forecast.

## 4. What the valuation methods say — approximately 4 minutes

[Show the valuation table in the detailed outline. All amounts below are USD, and share counts are in millions.]

My earlier DCF and later linked model use different assumptions and valuation methods, so I need to explain their differences rather than average the answers.

The earlier DCF was prepared on September 10. It forecasts free cash flow to the firm, discounts it using a provisional 18% weighted average cost of capital, and assumes 3% terminal growth. The result is $2.1433 per share using 530.664781 million historical weighted-average shares.

Its enterprise value is about $192.6 million. Adding $1,098.8 million of cash and securities and subtracting the $154.1 million debt proxy gives about $1,137.3 million of equity value. The debt proxy and historical share denominator are important limitations, especially because of convertible debt.

The later linked model values free cash flow to equity directly, using a 15% cost of equity and 3% terminal growth. That 15% is a judgment assumption; it is not the same discount-rate estimate as the earlier 18% WACC.

The classroom convention that values positive forecast cash flows produces $1.23 per share. My sensitivity analysis instead uses a funding-adjusted method that includes the negative cash-flow years and adds starting liquidity above the required reserve. That result is about $2.00 per share, using 619.894349 million scenario shares.

The signed method includes about $998.8 million of starting excess liquidity, $41.7 million of discounted positive cash flow, negative $520.7 million of discounted cash outflows, and $721.2 million of discounted terminal value. Together, these produce about $1,241 million of equity value.

Because this is already an equity cash-flow model with the debt treatment included, I do not subtract the converted or repaid debt again. I also do not add ending cash again or count the sale of the initial securities twice.

The terminal value assumes the business reaches more sustainable investment needs after 2030. That is a major judgment. Terminal value supplies about 94.5% of the positive-cash-flow classroom valuation, showing how much that particular result depends on the long-run assumptions.

[Point to the dated market comparison in Lab 10.]

The saved September 24 quote was $74.63 at 1:46 PM Eastern. Using the disclosed comparison basis of 641.739433 million as-converted shares, the signed model value becomes about $1.93 per share. That adjustment only changes the denominator. It does not update the model for subsequent financing, acquisitions or operating results.

All of these model values discount from the year-end 2025 baseline using annual periods. They are not precise valuations on the September quote dates, and neither saved quote is today's price. The large gap raises questions about expectations and missing information; it does not prove that the market is wrong.

[Show the reverse-DCF paragraph in Lab 06.]

The earlier reverse DCF used the September 10 closing price of $61.96. It tested a uniform shift in all five forecast cash-flow margins, from minus five to plus ten percentage points. It held revenue, the discount rate, terminal growth, cash, debt and shares fixed. The changed final-year cash flow also changed terminal value.

The resulting endpoint values were about $1.30 and $3.84 per share. There was no solution for $61.96 within that range. This means that the tested margin changes could not explain the target price under the other fixed assumptions. It does not identify a market-implied growth rate.

[Show the peer decisions in Lab 08.]

For peers, I researched Redwire and Avio. Redwire overlaps with space systems, while Avio overlaps with launch and propulsion. Their business mix, geography and customers differ from Rocket Lab's, so neither is a perfect match.

Redwire had negative reported earnings. Avio had positive reported diluted earnings and a descriptive historical P/E of about 82.38 times. However, Rocket Lab's own annual earnings per share were negative. Applying a positive peer P/E to those losses does not create a meaningful target price. My peer result is therefore an explained limitation, not a zero valuation and not a second number to average with the DCF.

## 5. What drives the results — approximately 3 minutes

[Open `rocketlab_lab11_output.md`. Show the scenario table and higher-R&D trace.]

I tested revenue growth and R&D spending separately. For growth, I moved each year's growth rate up or down by five percentage points. For R&D, I increased or decreased each year's spending by 20%. All other independent assumptions stayed at base while the linked statements recalculated.

The base 2030 operating profit is about $268.8 million, and free cash flow to equity is about $84 million. The signed valuation is $2.002 per share on the 619.894349 million scenario-share basis.

Lower revenue growth produces a value of $1.338 per share; higher growth produces $2.760. Lower R&D produces $2.631, while higher R&D produces $1.357. These are the same annual-baseline valuation convention, not current price targets.

Growth has the larger low-to-high value span: about $1.423 per share compared with $1.274 for R&D. Growth changes compound through the revenue base and affect gross profit, administration costs and working capital.

Here is a specific example of how R&D moves through the statements. In 2030, base R&D is $330 million. Increasing it by 20% takes it to $396 million, an extra $66 million of expense. Operating profit therefore falls by $66 million.

Pretax income remains positive in that year. At the model's 25% tax rate, taxes fall by $16.5 million. Net income therefore falls by $49.5 million. Working capital, depreciation, capex and revenue are unchanged in this test, so free cash flow also falls by $49.5 million, from $83.956 million to $34.456 million.

Value per share falls from $2.002 to $1.357 because higher R&D affects the full forecast and the terminal calculation. Earlier loss years do not receive an immediate modeled tax benefit, so the 25% offset does not apply to every year.

The ranking is conditional on the tested ranges. Five percentage points of growth and 20% of R&D are different types of changes. This analysis does not establish that growth is always more important, that it is the most uncertain input, or that the scenarios have particular probabilities.

The biggest R&D limitation is that lower spending improves the modeled numbers without reducing future technical success or revenue. Real development spending can create future benefits. Therefore, this test does not show that Rocket Lab should cut R&D. A full development-delay scenario could also change revenue timing, capital spending and funding needs.

The saved checks passed in every scenario, and restoring the base reproduced the original results exactly. The lowest cash balance across the scenarios was about $211.4 million, above the $100 million floor.

## 6. What would change my conclusion — approximately 2 minutes

My conclusion remains conditional: watch and wait while investigating whether the operating forecast is supportable.

The next assumption I would research is whether the revenue path can be achieved with the modeled R&D and capital spending. I would look for evidence about development milestones, reliable delivery, production costs and the conversion of contracts into revenue and cash.

Delays or cost overruns would make me reconsider the growth path, spending needs and funding together. Evidence of dependable execution and improving unit economics would support revisiting the forecast. I would also update cash, debt, dilution and the valuation date before making a comparison with a current market price.

The main lesson from the analysis is that revenue growth does not automatically create cash for shareholders. The spending required to produce that growth, the timing of cash generation and the eventual share count all matter.

I would like the review to focus on whether my growth and margin assumptions are consistent with the investment I modeled, and whether my long-run cash-flow assumptions are defensible.

## Prepared answers for the question period

These are practice questions and draft answers, not questions already received from a partner.

**Why did you use such high growth?** The path tests continued expansion at slowing rates, starting near the saved 2025 reported growth rate. That provides context, not proof. I need delivery and contract-conversion evidence to defend the forecast, and I should not describe all reported growth as organic.

**Why does gross margin reach 43%?** It is a judgment about scale and mix. The direction may be plausible, but the exact endpoint needs support. Weak utilization, launch delays or manufacturing overruns would challenge it.

**Why not simply buy because revenue is growing?** Revenue alone does not show that future cash flows justify the price. My analysis needs a consistent, updated valuation and evidence supporting the operating assumptions.

**Why not sell because your valuation is much lower than the quote?** This annual-baseline scenario omits later developments and retains significant forecast and capitalization limitations. The gap alone does not establish a current investment recommendation.

**Why are there several values per share?** The earlier FCFF model and later FCFE model differ in forecast, discount rate, financing treatment and shares. The later model also separates the positive-only classroom convention from the signed-cash-flow method. Each number must stay attached to its method and basis.

**Why does more R&D lower taxes by only $16.5 million?** The 2030 expense increase is $66 million. With positive pretax income and a 25% modeled tax rate, the tax reduction is 66 × 25% = $16.5 million. The remaining $49.5 million reduces net income and FCFE.

**Does the model predict Neutron's success?** No. Total company R&D is a cost input, not a model of launch success or a separately disclosed Neutron budget. The revenue effects of development outcomes are not linked automatically.

**What if you cannot answer a question?** I would identify the precise missing evidence or calculation and record how to resolve it. I would not treat a proposed change as a completed model repair.
